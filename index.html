Taming Performance Variability at Exascale: Mitigating OS Noise on El Capitan

Speaker: Edgar Leon
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory

Abstract

Exascale systems like El Capitan deliver unprecedented computing power, but their scale and architectural complexity also introduce new performance and variability challenges, especially system noise caused by operating system services and background activity. In this talk, Edgar Leon will highlight a collaboration between Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE) to address system noise on El Capitan, formerly the world’s fastest verified supercomputer. Through OS kernel tuning, system service isolation, and benchmark-driven design, LLNL and HPE developed a distribution-independent, Linux-based mitigation strategy that significantly improves application scalability and reproducibility. Techniques such as CPU and core specialization, interrupt affinity control, and I/O and huge pages management were evaluated and optimized. As a result, several science workloads, including Pennant, Nekbone, and AMG, achieved performance improvements of up to 145% at scale.

Bio

Leon is a computer scientist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), where he leads research and development in high-performance computing (HPC) to improve the performance, productivity, and portability of scientific applications for next-generation supercomputing systems. His research focuses on operating systems, distributed systems, computer architecture, performance portability, and emerging memory technologies. Prior to joining LLNL, he held research positions at IBM Research and Sandia National Laboratories. Dr. Leon earned his Ph.D. in Computer Science from the University of New Mexico and is a Senior Member of the IEEE in recognition of his contributions to the HPC community.

Julia meets the Intelligence Processing Unit

Speaker: Mose Giordano
University College London

Abstract

The Julia ecosystem developed many ways to interface GPUs, ranging from low-level vendor-specific packages, to high-level abstractions for generic vendor-agnostic GPU programming. But the ability to use accelerators is not limited to GPUs: by leveraging the LLVM framework, it is possible to run Julia code on completely different devices as well. This includes the Intelligence Processing Unit (IPU), an accelerator for massively parallel workflows developed by Graphcore and powered by 1472 cores. The IPU is mainly employed in machine-learning applications through the optimised libraries developed by Graphcore, but the flexibility of this processor makes it suitable also for many other numerical programs. Julia offers the unique opportunity to write high-level custom code that can be compiled down to native code for the IPU, without having to directly interface the SDK provided by Graphcore. In this talk I will present simple benchmarks of Julia code on the IPU, and several applications involving DifferentialEquations.jl, and automatic differentiation via Enzyme.jl. I will also showcase unusual hardware features available on the IPU, such as stochastic rounding.

Bio

Mose Giordano is a Research Software Engineer at the University College London and a long time user of the Julia programming language. His interest is in testing, benchmarking and porting Julia to novel machines and hardware.

From HPC to HPCQC: Software Foundations for Hybrid Classical–Quantum Computing

Speaker: Martin Schulz
Technische Universität München (TUM)

Abstract

The evolution of quantum computing is reshaping the landscape of high-performance computing, where quantum processors are increasingly viewed as accelerators complementing classical architectures. Successfully transitioning from HPC to hybrid HPCQC systems requires software solutions that bridge established HPC workflows with rapidly evolving quantum technologies. Within the Munich Quantum Valley (MQV), we are addressing this challenge through the open-source Munich Quantum Software Stack (MQSS) and the Quantum Device Management Interface (QDMI), a vendor-independent interface for quantum hardware integration. This talk presents our vision and practical approach to building a sustainable software foundation for HPCQC, discusses key integration challenges and lessons learned, and outlines the current status and future potential of hybrid classical–quantum computing infrastructures.

Approaches and Challenges in Porting Scientific Applications to the Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine

Speaker: Piotr Łuczyński
Cerebras Systems

Abstract

With the growing demand of AI workloads in training and inference the development of novel hardware accelerators also has gotten more attention, as it promises greater compute-throughput and power efficiency. The Cerebras CS-3 is one such hardware innovation: the system is built from the ground up to accelerate large AI training and inference workloads. The CS-3 is built around the wafer-scale engine (WSE), a single chip consisting of 900,000 processing elements (PEs) laid out in a 2D mesh, with each PE containing 48 kB of memory accessible in a single cycle. While this chip has been explicitly designed to accelerate AI workloads, many of its features also address bottlenecks present in more traditional HPC workloads though might present additional challenges. In this talk, we explore the methodology and challenges of mapping traditional HPC workloads to the Cerebras WSE, using the Livermore Unstructured Lagrangian Explicit Shock Hydrodynamics (LULESH) proxy application as a case study.

Lamellar: Enabling Memory-Safe Distributed HPC in Rust

Speaker: Ryan D. Friese
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory

Abstract

High-performance computing has long depended on programming environments that prioritize performance over memory safety, data-race freedom, and software maintainability. As systems grow in scale and complexity, this tradeoff is increasingly difficult to justify. Rust, with its compile-time safety guarantees and zero-cost abstractions, offers a compelling foundation for building system software that is both safe and efficient. A question remains though, what does it look like to build core distributed runtime infrastructure for HPC in Rust? This talk presents Lamellar, an asynchronous distributed runtime developed at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory to explore that question. Lamellar supports large-scale distributed-memory applications while leveraging Rust's ownership model, type system, and async execution to improve correctness and productivity. I will discuss the design principles behind Lamellar, how Rust's safety guarantees apply in a distributed setting, and the practical challenges of bringing Rust into HPC system software—including interoperability with established environments and the tension between expressive abstractions and predictable performance. Beyond the specifics of Lamellar, the talk considers what Rust may mean for the future of HPC software more broadly, arguing that safety, performance, and productivity need not be competing objectives but can be pursued together as first-class design goals.

His research interests span hardware/software co-design of runtime and system software for novel architectures, HPC (high performance computing) network simulation and modeling, the analysis and optimization of data movement in large scale distributed workflows, and performance modeling of irregular applications. Distributed-Memory Real-Space Parallel DMRG for Large-Scale Matrix Product States

Speaker: Matthew Blomquist
National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC)

Abstract

We present performance results of a real-space parallel density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) algorithm on the Perlmutter supercomputer. Real-space parallel DMRG distributes the computational workload across sites of the matrix product state (MPS), and has primarily been used in the literature to add parallelism to the standard two-site DMRG. In this work, we leverage the real-space parallel approach to create a distributed-memory framework for performing DMRG on very large MPS that exceed the memory capacity of a single node. We show scaling results for one- and two-dimensional Heisenberg models, provide insights into load balancing, and detail the implementation considerations necessary to run the code at scale. Finally, we describe how this work aligns with the NERSC Science Acceleration Program (NESAP) for the Doudna supercomputer and how our Julia-based implementation leverages JACC.jl to support performance-portable and scalable execution on next-generation hardware.

He received his Ph.D. from the University of California, Merced. Scientific research through the lens of qutrits: Neutrino flavor oscillations and discrete time crystals

Speaker: Ermal Rrapaj
National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC)

Abstract

Generally operated as qubits, transmons have readily addressable higher levels, making them natural candidates for operation as quantum three-level systems (qutrits).Ternary quantum information processing in superconducting devices poses a promising alternative to its more popular binary counterpart through larger, more connected computational spaces and proposed advantages in quantum simulation and error correction. Recent works in transmon devices have realized high fidelity single qutrit operation. Nonetheless, effectively engineering a high-fidelity two-qutrit entanglement remains a central challenge for realizing qutrit processing in a transmon device, recently implemented at the Advanced Quantum Testbed (AQT) at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory. Using these technology, I present recent results in two different application areas: collective neutrino flavor oscillations and discrete time crystals. Collective neutrino flavor oscillations are of primary importance in understanding the dynamic evolution of core-collapse supernovae and subsequent terrestrial detection, but also among the most challenging aspects of numerical simulations. This situation is complicated by the quantum many-body nature of the problem due to neutrino-neutrino interactions which demands a quantum treatment. An additional complication is the presence of three flavors, which often is approximated by the electron flavor and a heavy lepton flavor. We provide both qubit and qutrit encodings for all three flavors, and develop optimized quantum circuits for the time evolution and analyze the Trotter error. Periodically driven quantum many-body systems can spontaneously break discrete time-translation symmetry, realizing discrete time crystals. To date, both experimental and theoretical efforts have largely focused on the simplest case of spontaneous period-doubling in Z2 discrete time crystals realized with qubits. This owes, in part, to the challenge of stabilizing eigenstate order in higher discrete symmetry time crystals, due to the presence of richer domain wall physics. Here, we demonstrate the realization of a Z3 discrete time crystal by implementing a Floquet chiral clock model in a chain of 15 superconducting qutrits. Unlike the conventional Ising setting, our system features a tunable chiral angle that governs domain-wall dynamics, spectral degeneracies, and crucially, the stability of time-crystalline order.

Bio

Ermal Rrapaj is a staff member of the Advanced Technologies Group (ATG) in computational research at Berkeley Lab. Prior to joining ATG, Ermal was a Riken-Berkeley fellow and N3AS fellow. He is a visiting researcher at the University of California Berkeley Physics Department and Interdisciplinary Theoretical and Mathematical Sciences Program (iTHEMS) RIKEN. Rrapaj's work at NERSC explores the intersection of scientific computing algorithms, deep learning methods for science, HPC system architecture, and quantum computing applications.

Evolving Classical PDE Solvers: A Grammar-based Approach Using Evolutionary Algorithms

Speaker: Dinesh Parthasarathy
Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg

Abstract

We present a grammar-based approach that uses context-free grammars to infuse domain knowledge and structure into the design of classical PDE solvers. A population of candidate solver programs is evolved using evolutionary algorithms, with the search constrained by the grammar rules. In contrast to machine learning approaches that rely on differentiable codes to learn optimal solver components, we adopt a complementary strategy: employing evolutionary algorithms to construct efficient solvers from proven algorithmic building blocks. This has a key practical advantage—enabling easy and fairly non-intrusive integration with existing solver frameworks such as hypre. Numerical experiments on scientific applications using hypre demonstrate the potential of our approach for automated solver design.

Enzyme: High-Performance, Cross-Language, and Parallel Automatic Differentiation

Speaker: William Moses
University of Illinois

Abstract

Automatic differentiation (AD) is key to training neural networks, Bayesian inference, and scientific computing. Applying these techniques requires rewriting code in a specific machine learning framework or manually providing derivatives. This talk presents Enzyme, a high-performance automatic differentiation compiler plugin for the LLVM and MLIR compiler frameworks. Enzyme differentiates programs in any language whose compiler targets LLVM/MLIR, including C/C++, Fortran, Julia, Rust, Swift, JaX, etc., thereby providing native AD capabilities in these languages with state-of-the-art performance. Unlike traditional tools, Enzyme performs AD on optimized IR. On a combined machine-learning and scientific computing benchmark suite, AD on optimized IR achieves a geometric mean speedup of 4.2x over AD on IR before optimization. This talk will also include work that makes Enzyme the first fully automatic reverse-mode AD tool to generate gradients of existing GPU kernels as well as the benefits of operating within high-level structured representations, like MLIR.

Bio

William Moses is an Assistant Professor at the University of Illinois in the Computer Science and Electrical and Computer Engineering departments. He received a Ph.D. in Computer Science from MIT, where he also received his M.Eng in electrical engineering and computer science (EECS) and B.S. in EECS and physics. Transparent Resource Adaptivity for Task-Based Applications on Supercomputers

Speaker: Jonas Posner
University of Kassel, Germany
Substitute Chair - Software Engineering

Abstract

Traditional static resource allocation in supercomputers (jobs retain a fixed set of resources) leads to inefficiencies. Resource adaptivity (jobs can change resources at runtime) significantly increases supercomputer efficiency.
This talk will exploit Asynchronous Many-Task (AMT) programming, which is well suited for adaptivity due to its transparent resource management. An AMT runtime system dynamically assigns user-defined small tasks to workers to achieve load balancing and adapt to resource changes.
